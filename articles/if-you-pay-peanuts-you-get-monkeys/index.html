If you pay peanuts, you get monkeys!

Commission-only sounds free. It rarely is. Why a low upfront cost and good value are not the same thing in a service business.

Olivier Attia, Founder, Gershon ConsultingNovember 24, 20263 min read
Listen · podcast version
4 min 28 s · Download MP3
If you pay peanuts, you get monkeys.

It’s a blunt expression. But in a service business, it points to a mistake that can become very expensive: confusing a low upfront cost with good value.

We hire lawyers, accountants, consultants and other specialists because their expertise should help us achieve something valuable: save money, make money, save time, avoid mistakes or make better decisions.

The question should be: “Is the service worth what I’m paying?”

Yet we regularly hear: “We only work on commission. If you won’t accept that, we’ll find someone who will.”

You probably will. But what exactly are you getting?

An introduction and a professional service are different things

If someone makes an occasional introduction, with little preparation and no responsibility for the work that follows, a commission can make perfect sense. They open a door and receive a reward if business results.

But delivering a professional service can involve:

  • Researching the market and identifying suitable prospects.
  • Defining the offer and positioning it correctly.
  • Investing in tools, databases and systems.
  • Qualifying buyers before arranging meetings.
  • Managing outreach, follow-ups and objections.
  • Coordinating negotiations and supporting the closing process.

That takes expertise, resources and sustained work, whether a transaction closes next week, six months from now or never.

Commission can reward a successful outcome. It does not automatically fund everything needed to produce one.

“It costs you nothing” is rarely true

The promise is familiar: “This will be easy.” “I know exactly the right buyers.” “I just need to make a few calls.”

Then the weeks pass. Introductions lead nowhere. Prospects turn out to have no budget, no authority or no serious intention to buy.

Six months later, you may have paid no commission. But you have spent hours on meetings, explanations, proposals and follow-ups. You have also lost six months of opportunity.

During that time, competitors kept working. Potential clients made decisions. Your team’s attention was tied up in conversations that never should have reached your calendar.

Ten percent of zero is zero.

But the cost of getting there can be substantial.

There’s another saying: “If you think hiring a professional is expensive, try hiring an amateur.”

A good professional cannot guarantee every sale. They should, however, bring a credible process, relevant expertise, clear responsibilities and accountability for the work they deliver.

Commission-only arrangements can work when the economics, responsibilities and control over the outcome support them. But someone accepting commission-only terms is no proof that they can deliver. Someone charging a fee is no guarantee either.

Look at the substance: their track record, their process, their resources and the work they actually commit to doing.

Our position

For our business, the position is straightforward: we charge for the professional work required to deliver our service. A success fee may be part of an agreement, but we do not fund the entire assignment through commission alone.

Our time, expertise and tools have value before the deal closes.

Share your own stories. Have you spent months following “great introductions” that led nowhere? What did those introductions actually cost you, and what would you do differently today?
